## The assumption I set out to test

The global literature keeps asking whether health workers will accept AI. In Buea that question was being answered by assumption, mostly by people who have never worked a shift in a Cameroonian regional hospital.

## How I collected it

419 questionnaires across six cadres at Buea Regional Hospital. 387 came back complete and consented, a 92.4% response rate.

*[What the fieldwork was actually like. How long it took. What people said when you handed them the form.]*

I built the instrument myself rather than adapting an existing one, because the existing ones test whether you recognise computer science vocabulary. That is not the same as knowing what the technology does on a ward.

## What they knew

Mean knowledge score of 61.3%. Strong on concrete uses like AI reading medical images at 75.2%. Weak on governance: only 41.3% knew where Cameroon stands on AI legislation.

That gap is not really an ignorance finding. There genuinely is a policy vacuum. They are describing reality accurately.

## What they felt

30.7% positive, 66.9% moderate, 2.3% negative.

The two numbers that matter most sit next to each other: 76.5% want training before AI is introduced, and the weakest item in the whole attitude section was trust that AI recommendations are accurate.

*[Your reading of this. The clinical instinct behind withholding trust from a recommendation you cannot verify.]*

## The finding that reframed it for me

I asked what would actually influence their use of AI. Eighteen factors.

Top: reliable electricity, at 94.8%. Then cost, internet, equipment.

Last, out of all eighteen: fear of losing their job.

Meanwhile 95.6% own a smartphone, 33.6% have reliable internet at work, and 12.9% have ever had AI training.

## What I think it means

They are not resistant. They are under-trained and under-equipped, and they know it.
